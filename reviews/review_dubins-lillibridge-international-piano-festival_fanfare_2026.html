Madeleine Forte, Ildikó Bartha, János Kéry, pn

The Lillibridge Ensemble

By now, from a number of glowing reviews in the Fanfare Archive, readers of this journal are (or should be) familiar with Madeleine Forte and the Lillibridge Ensemble. A Juilliard-trained pianist and former student of pianists Alfred Cortot and Wilhelm Kempff, Madeleine founded the ensemble to foster cross-cultural artistic exchange, assembling top-tier musicians from across the United States, France, Hungary, Poland, and Israel to collaborate in the performance of chamber music at the highest artistic level.

The ensemble has established a strong presence in Connecticut and New England’s classical music scenes, performing at major community venues and schools, including the Neighborhood Music School in New Haven and the Westminster Presbyterian Church in West Hartford. And in a joint venture with Roméo Records, many of the Lillibridge’s performances have been issued on CD, as well as having been filmed and made available for streaming on various platforms, including YouTube.

The Lillibridge International Piano Festival, which was initially established in 2018, was a natural spinoff that grew out of the ensemble’s “live” concert events. Unlike other annual music festivals—e.g. Aspen and Tanglewood—which are named for their geographic locations, one could say that the Lillibridge Piano Festival is a musical moveable feast, for it does perambulate a bit to various locales and venues. For example, in August and September 2023, The Lillibridge Ensemble performed in a three-day International Music Festival in New York City at Klavierhaus. Those performances can also be viewed and heard on YouTube.

The recording under review is the audio-only outtake from the Festival’s 2025 event, which took place on October 3rd at Evergreen Woods in North Branford, CT. A review of this program in its streamed YouTube video format is also published in parallel by a Fanfare colleague.

As is customary with releases of this sort, where multiple composers and multiple performers are involved, the above headnote lists the composers in alphabetical order as opposed to recording/track order. And so, we begin with the one composer neither I nor the Fanfare Archive has ever heard of until now. But read on for the surprise. American composer, concert pianist and harpsichordist, and Assistant Professor of Piano at the University of Michigan, Matthew Bengtson was born in 1974. His professional biography states that he is Philadelphia-based and holds musical degrees from the Peabody Institute in Baltimore and from Harvard University.

His extended bio on his personal website tells us that Bengtson is an advocate for both contemporary and rarely performed music and has a diverse repertoire, ranging from William Byrd to numerous contemporary composers with whom he actively collaborates. He has performed concerts as both soloist and collaborator in France, Germany, Italy, Poland, and Hungary, and in Mexico, Washington, DC, at Monticello, and in solo recitals at Carnegie Hall’s Weill Recital Hall. He has been heard with violinist Joshua Bell on NPR’s Performance Today and XM Satellite Radio’s Classical Confidential. His recordings can be heard on various labels, including Roméo. Perhaps of even more interest to some readers is that Bengtson has become an acknowledged specialist in the music of Scriabin and has recorded the composer’s complete piano sonatas for Roméo Records.

And here’s the surprise. It turns out that Matthew Bengtson is known to the pages of Fanfare after all, but as an instrumentalist/performer rather than as a composer. He was interviewed by John Bell Young in issue 38:6, and his Scriabin recordings were reviewed in that issue by a number of the magazine’s contributors.

Turnabout being fair play, as a composer, Bengtson has written a piece for solo piano titled A la manière de Ravel, as a rejoinder perhaps, to Ravel’s A la manière de Borodine and A la manière de Chabrier. To my ear, it seems to embrace a number of styles over the course of its 4:41 duration. The opening bars suggest a nocturne by Chopin. A bit further on, the piece begins to resemble later Scriabin, which is to say it becomes quite dissonant and rhythmically disjointed. Tonality of the pentatonic persuasion is restored during a brief visit from Debussy, and at midpoint there comes a beautiful Romantic interlude that could pass for one of Rachmaninoff’s Moments Musicaux. Ravel finally makes an appearance with a flash of Spain and the Basque folk idioms that had a strong influence on his music.

Over the span of four minutes and 41 seconds, pianist Madeleine Forte gives a kaleidoscopic performance of Bengtson’s piece that brings out the music’s rainbow of motley colors and textures. Then, in deference to her pianist friends and associates, she cedes the stage to them for the rest of the program, to be heard only once more, partnering with János Kéry in an arrangement for piano four hands of Manuel de Falla’s Spanish Dance No. 1 from La vida breve.

Of the remaining seven numbers, three of them—the Brahms Hungarian Dance and the Dumka and Furiant from Dvořák’s Slavonic Dances—are performed in their original composer-scoring for piano four hands. The three solo piano numbers by Liszt are played with tremendous technical panache by Kéry, while Ildikó Bartha, playing solo, casts a sparkling wintry spell, at once both magical and menacing, over Rachmaninoff’s G-Minor Prelude.

Bartha is a Hungarian pianist and pedagogue based in Hungary. She is on the piano faculty at the Zoltán Kodály Secondary Music School and is affiliated with the Liszt Ferenc Academy of Music. In addition to her teaching activities, Bartha is an engaging concert and recording artist, known primarily to audiences in her native Hungary, where she is heavily invested in exploring regional music history and lesser-known Hungarian composers. A special project she has undertaken is an analysis of the piano works of Robert Volkmann, a German-born composer who spent most of his creative life in Budapest.

Hungarian pianist János Kéry has toured more widely than Bartha beyond his native country’s borders, appearing in concert on a number of international stages. A curious coincidence is that Kéry was born in 1974, the same year as our first-listed composer above, Matthew Bengtson. With Kéry, at least we can add the “where” of his birth: Debrecen, Hungary.

His early musical education was at the Kodály Zoltán Conservatory, where he studied piano and composition. He then continued his studies at Boise State University in Idaho, before returning to Budapest to earn honors at the Liszt Ferenc Academy, which he then followed up doing postgraduate work and earning additional diplomas from the Saarbrücken University of Music in Germany.

Kéry is widely recognized for his scholarly work on the 19th-century Hungarian composer and pianist Imre Székely. He wrote his doctoral thesis on Székely’s life and works, compiling a detailed catalog of his Hungarian fantasies.

Bartha and Kéry make for a winning duo team, bringing out the Gypsy element in the Brahms Hungarian Dance with a real zest and zing for the idiom. Likewise, they flavor Dvořák’s two selections from his Slavonic Dances with just the right amounts of Czech seasonings.

Behind the scenes, as it were, Madeleine Forte is the organizer and musical mastermind, who has brought these wonderful artists together and made the dream of an international piano festival a reality. Her efforts are acknowledged and applauded, loudly and often.

Jerry Dubins
